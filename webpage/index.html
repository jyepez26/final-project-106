Does a Relaxed Test Taker Always Do Better than a Stressed One?

DSC 106 Final Project


Scroll down to continue

Test-taking is a universal experience that often comes with varying levels of stress. While some students thrive under pressure, others may find their performance hindered by anxiety. This project explores the relationship between stress levels and test performance through the lens of the Yerkes-Dodson Law, which suggests that performance increases with stress up to an optimal point, after which it decreases.

We measure stress through 3 key physiological indicators:

  1. Electrodermal Activity (EDA): Measures the electrical conductance of the skin, which increases with sweat production. This is a direct indicator of emotional arousal and stress levels.
  2. Heart Rate (HR): Tracks the number of heartbeats per minute, which typically increases during periods of stress or physical activity.
  3. Skin Surface Temperature (Temp): Tracks the body temperature (measured in °C) of test taker, which typically increases during stress or physical activity.

By analyzing these stress indicators during actual test-taking scenarios, we can gain insights into how stress patterns evolve throughout a test, whether there's a correlation between stress levels and test performance, if different students handle test stress differently, and what stress patterns might indicate optimal performance.

Explore the visualizations below to see how stress indicators vary across different students and tests, and discover what these patterns might tell us about the relationship between stress and performance.

Understanding the Results

You might have noticed that students with higher stress levels sometimes performed better than those with lower stress. This isn't a coincidence - it's explained by the Yerkes-Dodson Law, which shows the relationship between stress and performance.

Watch how performance changes with stress levels. Notice the "sweet spot" where moderate stress leads to optimal performance. This helps explain why some students perform better with higher stress - they might be operating closer to their optimal stress level.
